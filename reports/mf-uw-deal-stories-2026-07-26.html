The story

George Commons is the cleanest cash-flow buy in the pull, and it hides its catch in plain sight. A 7.8% stated in-place cap on a $75k/unit basis is a strong going-in yield for a stabilized-ish Midwest garden asset — and 223 days on market means the seller has already waited two-thirds of a year for a buyer who hasn't come. That is your leverage.

But the value-add math is honest only if you respect the exit. At a tertiary-market 8.0% exit cap, the renovation plan barely clears its own cost at full ask — so this is not an appreciation play. It's a yield play where entry discipline is the entire return. Underwrite it as: buy below ask, harvest a 9%+ in-place yield on your basis, and treat the rent lift as margin of safety, not the thesis.

One data flag before anyone falls in love: the export shows 15,360 sf against ~32 units — about 480 sf/unit, which doesn't reconcile with garden apartments. Either the unit count or the building SF is wrong. The rent roll settles it, and it changes everything downstream.

Local comps — original read

Vincennes is a ~16,000-person county seat whose demand floor is Vincennes University (~3,600 students) plus regional healthcare and light manufacturing. The comp set is thin and the renovated 2BR ceiling is realistically ~$850–900 — verify against VU off-campus product and Knox County workforce rentals before assuming any push.

How to get rents up

  • Light-to-moderate interior renovations (LVP, cabinets, counters, fixtures) to justify a market rent bump
  • Institute RUBS — bill back water/sewer/trash, a near-universal gap in mom-and-pop tertiary assets
  • Capture other income: laundry, pet fees, covered parking, application/admin
  • Close the physical-vs-economic occupancy gap with professional management and tighter collections

Key risks to underwrite

  • Tertiary demand depth — single-employer/university sensitivity
  • Soft exit cap (model 8%+); little appreciation cushion
  • Unit-count / building-SF mismatch must be resolved on the rent roll

The story

Briggs Flats is the only deal in the export where appreciation — not just yield — is a real story, and it's the only one missing the one number that matters. 550 Fletcher Ave sits on the near-southeast side, in the gravitational pull of Fountain Square and Bates-Hendricks — genuinely appreciating Indianapolis pockets with rent momentum most of the Midwest can't match.

A 7.3% stated cap on an operating building in a growth submarket is a call-the-broker-today signal. The catch is that CREXi lists no asking price, so the deal literally cannot be ranked until you have it. Everything hinges on the number against that cap and on exactly which side of the Fountain Square line the building sits.

Underwrite this one for the tailwind, not against it: Indianapolis has real employer diversity — Eli Lilly, Salesforce, Rolls-Royce, a deep logistics base — and the rent-growth history to support pushing a renovated unit rather than merely holding it.

Local comps — original read

Fletcher Ave (46203) is Fountain Square adjacency — the strongest micro-location in the pull. Metro workforce 2BR runs ~$1,050–1,250 depending on the block, and renovated infill near the square can reach higher. Original read: confirm the address is inside the appreciating ring, not one arterial removed — the rent delta across that line is large.

How to get rents up

  • Full interior renovations to reach Fountain Square-adjacent renovated rents, not just workforce median
  • Add in-unit or on-site laundry where absent — a real rent driver in urban infill
  • Lean into the rent-growth tailwind: mark leases to market on every turn
  • Other income (parking, storage, pet) scaled to a walkable urban tenant base

Key risks to underwrite

  • No asking price — deal cannot be scored until posted
  • Micro-location: must confirm the appreciating side of Fountain Square
  • Older infill building — verify mechanicals, roof, and any historic overlay

The story

Tiffany Square is the highest-effort, highest-conviction-on-basis play in the pull. At roughly $44 a square foot — about $36k a unit on an estimated ~72-unit garden complex — you are buying at a basis so far below replacement cost that the margin of safety is the deal. That is the canvas value-add is built on: cheap in, real scale, room to renovate and re-tenant.

The counterweight is Shreveport itself. The metro is soft, with flat-to-declining population, so market strength is the weak leg of the thesis. You are not riding a tailwind here — you are manufacturing every dollar of NOI through renovation, occupancy, and professional management in a mom-and-pop market. That's an operator's deal, not an allocator's.

The demand floor that makes it thinkable: Barksdale Air Force Base, the Bossier casino corridor, and the Cyber Innovation District put a workforce base under west Shreveport. Verify the Jewella Ave submarket's crime and real economic occupancy hard — at this basis the numbers seduce, and the submarket is where these deals are won or lost.

Local comps — original read

Shreveport-Bossier renovated garden 2BR realistically runs ~$800–900. The metro's demand is a floor, not an engine — Barksdale AFB and Bossier gaming anchor it, but there's no rent-growth story to lean on. Original read: underwrite flat rent growth and win on basis and execution, or don't do it at all.

How to get rents up

  • Full reposition — this is a renovation story, not a refresh; interiors, exteriors, and site
  • Professionalize management in a mom-and-pop market — the single biggest NOI lever here
  • Build other income from near-zero: RUBS, laundry, fees
  • Drive economic (not just physical) occupancy — collections and loss-to-lease discipline

Key risks to underwrite

  • Soft, flat-to-declining metro — no rent-growth tailwind
  • Heavy, execution-intensive capex (~$1.3M) — operator-dependent
  • Submarket (Jewella Ave / west Shreveport) crime & occupancy must be verified

  • Value creation — total basis (purchase + capex + closing & reserves) against stabilized value at a conservative exit cap. Where value created is thin, the return lives in going-in yield or entry basis, and the memo says so.
  • Timeline — an indicative 24-month stabilization plan; workstreams overlap by design.
